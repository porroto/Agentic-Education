## Abstract

Generative artificial intelligence is transforming how learners create, research, communicate, design, and demonstrate understanding. As AI systems become increasingly capable of producing polished academic and creative artifacts, education faces a critical challenge: When artificial intelligence can generate the final product, how can educators identify, cultivate, and validate authentic human learning? This concept paper proposes Agentic Education, a human-centered framework in which students, educators, families, communities, institutions, and AI systems participate in a shared learning ecosystem while preserving human agency, judgment, creativity, ethics, and responsibility. Rather than focusing only on whether students used AI, Agentic Education shifts attention toward the learning process: decisions, iterations, evidence, reflection, critique, and growth. The paper introduces the OpenLab Proof-of-Learning model as a possible framework for documenting and validating human learning in AI-assisted environments. Drawing from co-intelligence, constructivist learning theory, constructionism, sociocultural theory, and emerging AI competency frameworks, this paper argues that the future of education should not be organized around competing with AI or detecting AI use alone. Instead, education should help learners direct intelligent tools responsibly, document their process, and demonstrate human growth through evidence. As intelligence becomes increasingly abundant, human agency becomes a central curriculum.

**Keywords:** generative AI, agentic education, proof-of-learning, co-intelligence, student agency, AI literacy, portfolio assessment, human-AI collaboration

## Introduction

Generative artificial intelligence is changing the relationship between learners, knowledge, and production. Students can now use AI systems to draft essays, generate images, write code, summarize research, simulate conversations, create presentations, and receive feedback. These capabilities create both opportunity and disruption. AI can support creativity, accelerate learning, and expand access to sophisticated tools. At the same time, it can obscure authorship, weaken traditional assessment models, and make it harder to determine what a learner actually understands.

For much of modern schooling, assessment has relied on evaluating final artifacts. A written response, project, test, presentation, or design product was treated as evidence of learning. The assumption was that the student who produced the artifact had also developed the knowledge, skills, or reasoning represented by it. Generative AI weakens this assumption. A polished artifact may now represent deep learning, shallow prompting, or a mixture of human and machine contribution.

This paper argues that education needs a shift from output-based verification to process-based proof-of-learning. The central question should not be only, “Did the student use AI?” A more useful question is, “What happened to the student’s thinking, agency, and judgment while working with AI?” In this context, proof-of-learning refers to a structured record of human growth demonstrated through decisions, evidence, reflection, iteration, critique, and validation.

## The Challenge of AI-Generated Artifacts

Generative AI complicates traditional ideas of authorship and assessment. When a learner submits an artifact, educators must now consider multiple possibilities. The learner may have produced the work independently. The learner may have used AI as a tutor, editor, or collaborator. The learner may have outsourced most of the intellectual work to the system. The final product alone often cannot reveal which of these occurred.

This creates an authorship problem, but more importantly, it creates a learning evidence problem. If education focuses only on final products, it may reward students who can generate polished outputs without developing deep understanding. However, banning or avoiding AI does not solve the deeper issue. AI is becoming part of work, communication, science, design, and civic life. Learners need guidance in using AI responsibly rather than simply being told to avoid it.

Mollick (2024) described the emerging relationship between humans and AI as a form of co-intelligence, where humans and machines increasingly collaborate in creative and professional tasks. In this environment, the human role does not disappear. Instead, human judgment becomes more important. People must learn to define problems, evaluate quality, apply ethics, and decide what is worth creating.

## Human Agency in the Age of Abundant Intelligence

Agency refers to the capacity to act intentionally, make choices, pursue goals, reflect on outcomes, and shape one’s environment. In education, agency is not merely independence. It includes purpose, self-direction, metacognition, ethical awareness, and responsibility.

Generative AI increases the need for agency because it increases the number of possible outputs. When a system can generate many answers, designs, or explanations, the learner must decide which ones are meaningful, accurate, ethical, useful, or worth improving. In this sense, AI does not eliminate the need for human judgment. It raises the value of judgment.

Taste may become one of the most important educational capacities in the AI era. Taste is not only aesthetic preference. It includes the ability to recognize quality, relevance, coherence, originality, and meaning. In a classroom, taste appears when a learner can say, “This answer is not strong enough,” “This design does not solve the real problem,” or “This explanation sounds correct but does not match the evidence.”

## Theoretical Foundations

Agentic Education connects to several learning traditions. Dewey (1938) argued that education is grounded in experience and reflection. Learning is not simply the reception of information; it involves interaction with real problems and the reconstruction of experience. In an AI-supported classroom, this means learners should not only receive AI-generated answers. They should use AI within meaningful experiences that require inquiry, choice, and reflection.

Vygotsky’s (1978) sociocultural theory also provides a strong foundation. Learning develops through social interaction, mediation, language, and tools. AI can be understood as a new mediating tool in the learning environment. However, tools do not automatically produce learning. Learning depends on how tools are used within relationships, contexts, and guided activity.

Bruner (1960) emphasized the importance of structure, discovery, and scaffolding in education. AI systems can provide new forms of scaffolding, but scaffolding must support development rather than replace it. If AI completes the intellectual work for the learner, it may reduce growth. If AI helps the learner operate just beyond current ability while still requiring reflection and explanation, it can support development.

Papert’s (1980) constructionism is especially relevant. Papert argued that learners develop understanding by making meaningful public artifacts. In constructionist environments, students learn through designing, building, debugging, sharing, and revising. Generative AI can expand constructionist learning by helping students prototype ideas, simulate systems, and explore alternatives. However, the learning value still depends on the student’s active role in making, testing, and explaining.

## Defining Agentic Education

Agentic Education is a learning model in which students, teachers, families, communities, institutions, and AI systems participate in a shared learning ecosystem that supports human growth through purposeful creation, evidence, and validation.

In Agentic Education, students are not passive consumers of AI-generated content. They are builders, investigators, designers, and decision-makers. They use AI systems to support inquiry, creativity, and reflection, but they remain responsible for explaining their choices and demonstrating learning.

Teachers are not replaced by AI. They become validators, coaches, designers, and guardians of learning quality. Their role includes helping students ask better questions, interpret evidence, revise work, and understand ethical implications.

AI systems are not governors of learning. They assist. They may help generate ideas, organize information, simulate scenarios, provide feedback, or document evidence. However, final interpretation and validation remain human responsibilities.

The core principle of Agentic Education can be summarized as follows: **AI assists. Humans govern. Evidence speaks. Communities validate.**

## The OpenLab Proof-of-Learning Framework

OpenLab proposes a proof-of-learning framework designed to make human growth visible in AI-supported project-based learning. Instead of evaluating only final artifacts, OpenLab emphasizes the learning trail that produced them.

The model includes seven elements: mission, creation, evidence, AI assistance, reflection, human validation, and recognition.

First, learners begin with a mission. A mission is a real-world challenge, inquiry, or design problem. It may involve environmental science, robotics, sustainability, community problem-solving, AI literacy, or creative production.

Second, learners create. They research, design, build, test, revise, and communicate. AI may be used as a collaborator, tutor, simulator, critic, or organizer.

Third, learners collect evidence. Evidence may include drafts, prototypes, data tables, design notes, peer feedback, teacher comments, reflection logs, images of work, code, presentations, or explanations.

Fourth, AI assists with organization. AI agents may help summarize evidence, identify missing pieces, create reflection prompts, connect artifacts to rubric criteria, and prepare teacher review packets.

Fifth, learners reflect. Reflection is essential because it reveals the learner’s decision-making process. Students explain what they tried, what changed, what failed, what improved, and what they now understand.

Sixth, humans validate. Teachers, peers, mentors, families, or community partners evaluate whether the evidence demonstrates meaningful learning. AI may support this process, but it should not replace human judgment.

Seventh, learners receive recognition. Recognition may take the form of portfolio entries, badges, certificates, exhibitions, student-led conferences, or other forms of non-financial proof-of-learning.

In this model, the final artifact is not the only proof. The transformation process becomes the proof.

## From AI Detection to Learning Verification

Much of the early response to generative AI in schools has focused on detection. Detection tools attempt to determine whether text was written by AI. While understandable, this approach has limitations. It can produce false accusations, encourage adversarial behavior, and keep the focus on policing rather than learning.

Agentic Education suggests a different approach: learning verification. Rather than asking only whether AI was used, educators can ask students to document how AI was used. This requires process evidence.

A student might submit a project with a learning evidence record that includes the original question, AI prompts, rejected ideas, teacher feedback, peer critique, revised drafts, data collected, and final reflection. This does not eliminate academic integrity concerns, but it changes the educational conversation. Students are expected to show their thinking, not merely submit a product.

## The Teacher as Human Trust Layer

In AI-supported learning environments, teachers become more important, not less. If AI can generate content, then teaching cannot be reduced to content delivery. The teacher’s role shifts toward designing learning experiences, evaluating process evidence, guiding ethical use, and supporting student agency.

Teachers help learners answer questions such as: Why does this problem matter? What evidence supports your claim? How did your idea change? What did AI help with? What did you decide yourself? What would you improve next?

These questions are difficult for AI systems to answer on behalf of the learner because they require personal reflection, context, responsibility, and meaning. Teachers help students connect production with understanding.

## AI Agents and the Future of Learning Systems

As AI systems become more agentic, they may take on more complex roles in education. Future systems may include research agents, tutor agents, simulation agents, feedback agents, portfolio agents, and assessment support agents. These agents may help students and teachers manage complex learning workflows.

However, agentic systems require governance. AI agents should not become hidden authorities over student learning. Their roles should be transparent, bounded, and accountable. They should support documentation and reflection, not make high-stakes decisions without human review.

An OpenLab-style agentic workflow might include a portfolio curator agent that organizes evidence, a reflection agent that prompts students to explain decisions, an alignment agent that maps evidence to learning goals, and a review agent that flags missing evidence. The teacher would remain the final validator.

## Alignment With Emerging AI Competency Frameworks

International organizations are increasingly emphasizing AI literacy, ethics, agency, and responsible use. UNESCO’s AI competency frameworks for students and teachers emphasize that AI education should be human-centered, ethical, inclusive, and connected to agency and social responsibility (UNESCO, 2024a, 2024b).

Agentic Education aligns with this direction by treating AI literacy as more than technical skill. Students need to understand how AI works, but they also need to practice judgment, transparency, critique, collaboration, and reflection. Teachers need support in designing these experiences and validating learning in ways that preserve human dignity and agency.

A proof-of-learning approach can help operationalize these competencies. Instead of treating AI ethics or agency as abstract goals, educators can ask students to provide evidence of responsible AI use, revision, explanation, and human decision-making.

## OpenLab as Civic Learning Infrastructure

The long-term vision of OpenLab is not only a classroom tool. It is a civic learning infrastructure. Students complete real-world missions, collect evidence, receive human validation, and build portfolios that show growth over time.

This matters especially for underserved students. Traditional systems often fail to capture creativity, problem-solving, community impact, bilingual ability, technical experimentation, and resilience. A proof-of-learning system could help students make these forms of growth visible.

## Proposed Pilot Study

A future OpenLab pilot could be conducted in an elementary or middle-grade STEM classroom. Students would complete a project-based mission involving environmental science, micro:bit sensors, sustainability, or AI literacy. Students would be allowed to use AI tools under teacher guidance. Instead of submitting only a final product, students would maintain a learning evidence record.

Data sources could include student reflections, project artifacts, teacher notes, peer feedback, AI interaction summaries, rubric scores, and portfolio entries. An AI evidence assistant could help organize materials and generate teacher review packets. The teacher would validate final learning claims.

The study could examine whether the proof-of-learning process helps students better explain their thinking, whether teachers find the evidence useful, and what kinds of AI support improve or weaken learning.

Because student data and privacy are central concerns, the pilot should use de-identified artifacts, parent or guardian consent when required, and clear boundaries around AI use. The system should avoid automated high-stakes decisions.

## Ethical Considerations

AI-supported proof-of-learning systems must be designed carefully. Several risks must be addressed.

First, student privacy must be protected. Learning records may contain sensitive information, and students should not be placed under unnecessary surveillance. Evidence collection should be purposeful, minimal, and transparent.

Second, AI outputs may be biased or inaccurate. Teachers should not rely on AI recommendations without review. AI should flag uncertainty rather than pretend confidence.

Third, proof-of-learning systems should not become another mechanism of inequality. Students with greater access to devices, language support, or adult help may produce stronger evidence records. Schools must design equitable supports.

Fourth, AI collaboration should not erase human authorship. Students should receive recognition for their decisions, effort, reflection, and growth. AI contributions should be acknowledged, but human learning must remain central.

Finally, recognition systems such as badges or credentials should avoid financialization in K-12 settings. Student learning recognition should be non-financial, non-tradable, and focused on growth.

## Discussion

Generative AI forces education to reconsider what counts as learning evidence. If schools continue to evaluate only final products, they may struggle to distinguish between authentic learning and automated production. However, if schools shift toward process evidence, AI may become an opportunity to strengthen reflection, creativity, and agency.

Agentic Education offers a way forward. It does not reject AI, and it does not surrender learning to AI. Instead, it treats AI as a powerful collaborator that must be directed by human purpose and evaluated through evidence.

This shift changes the role of students. They become active designers of learning journeys. It changes the role of teachers. They become validators of growth and designers of meaningful learning systems. It changes the role of assessment. Assessment becomes less about catching students and more about helping them show how they developed.

## Conclusion

The future of education is not a competition between humans and artificial intelligence. It is a design challenge. Artificial intelligence can increase human capability, but capability alone does not create wisdom, purpose, or responsibility.

As AI systems generate more of the world’s content, education must help learners become more human, not less. Students need opportunities to ask meaningful questions, make decisions, test ideas, reflect on evidence, and use intelligent tools responsibly.

When answers become abundant, questions become valuable. When creation becomes easy, taste becomes scarce. When intelligence becomes everywhere, human agency becomes the curriculum.

Agentic Education and the OpenLab Proof-of-Learning model offer a path toward that future: AI expands possibility, humans provide direction, evidence preserves the journey, and learning remains human.
